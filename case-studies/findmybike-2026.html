FindMyBike, rebuilt: recovery as an agentic workflow

Then2015: a two-week concept — track a stolen bike, report it, share to social, sound the alarm. My role: UX design + research.
The real quote“I commute every day to class. I constantly worry about my bike being stolen.” — Marie, cyclist commuter, from the original research
Now2026 concept rebuild: the map becomes an agent that runs the recovery playbook — with a human checkpoint at every step
HonestyConcept study, labeled. 2015 facts are history; 2026 decisions are design reasoning, not a shipped product.
The 30-second version
ProblemThe 2015 app answered WHERE the bike is. It never answered the question that matters at the worst moment of a cyclist’s week: what do I do NOW? Worse — a live dot invites the owner to go confront a thief.
DecisionRecovery becomes an agent-run workflow: it drafts the police report, files the insurance claim, and verifies community sightings — every action previewed and approved by the owner, and the precise location routes to police, never to the owner’s fist.
OutcomeA working simulated prototype below — report the theft and run the recovery. Concept study; the 2015 original was my own project.

In 2015, FindMyBike put a dot on a map and an alarm on a bike. A decade later I’d call that design half-finished — because the dot answers the wrong question, and in the worst case it answers it dangerously.

01What we built then — and the flaw we didn’t name

The original was two focused weeks: research with real commuters (Marie’s worry above set the direction), market research into San Francisco’s theft economics — including a police department sitting on hundreds of recovered bikes it couldn’t return to owners for lack of identification — then flows, sketches, and a tracking prototype. The unexamined assumption: that knowing WHERE your bike is equals being able to get it back. It doesn’t. It mostly equals a terrible choice — do nothing with the dot, or follow it.

02The 2026 reframe: location is evidence, not a destination

Recovery is a workflow with institutional steps — police report with a serial number, insurance claim with documentation, community eyes with verification. Every step is bureaucratic, time-sensitive, and miserable to do while adrenalized. That’s exactly the shape of work an agent should run and a human should approve. The tracker’s coordinates stop being a destination for the owner and become evidence in a channel built to use it.

03The three design decisions

No live dot for the owner. This is the decision the whole rebuild hangs on. Owners see recovery status — report filed, sighting verified, police notified — while precise, current location routes to the police channel. The 2015 design quietly invited confrontation; the 2026 design makes de-escalation structural. Some users will hate this. It’s still right.

The agent drafts; the owner approves. Police report pre-filled from registration and tracker data; insurance claim assembled from receipts and photos; community alert composed without doxxing anyone. Each lands as a preview the owner confirms or edits — the confirm-what-you-see pattern, at the moment a person is least capable of paperwork and most in need of it.

Sightings must survive verification. A community report only surfaces when its photo matches the registered bike above a confidence threshold. Below it, nothing is shown and nobody is accused. False accusations are the community feature’s failure mode; the threshold is its k-anonymity.

The dot told owners where their anger should go. The agent tells the system where the evidence should go.

04The tension

Taking the live dot away from the person who owns the bike is paternalistic by design, and a real segment of users will experience it as the product siding with the thief. The counterweight is transparency: the owner sees everything the agent does, everything the police received, and every verified sighting — total visibility of the process, zero targeting information. Whether that trade survives contact with real users is precisely what a pilot would test.

The working notes

Why each rule exists, and what it costs.

EvidenceMethodConstraintLesson
EvidenceThe warehouse of unreturned bikes

The original 2015 research found the sharpest fact of the project: police departments recover bikes by the hundreds and can’t return them — owners can’t prove ownership, serials were never recorded. That fact survives into 2026 intact and reframes the product: the bottleneck was never locating bikes. It was connecting evidence to institutions. An agent that files a complete, serial-numbered report at minute one attacks the actual bottleneck.

MethodCheckpoints as the safety architecture

Every agent action — report, claim, community alert — renders as a preview the owner approves. The checkpoint does three jobs at once: it keeps the human legally accountable for what’s filed in their name, it catches model errors before they reach institutions, and it gives a panicking person a script. Agentic UX at its most useful is a checklist that fills itself out and still asks permission.

ConstraintThe paternalism trade, stated plainly

Withholding the live dot from the owner is a values decision wearing an interface. The design compensates with total process transparency, but the tension doesn’t dissolve — some riders want the dot and the risk. A concept study can hold that openly; a shipped product would need the pilot data to defend it. Listed as open, because it is.

Lesson2015 designed the sensor; 2026 designs the consequence

The decade’s pattern across this whole Rebuilt series: the old products surfaced information and stopped; the new ones take responsibility for what happens next. Koé gates who sees a voice; Compas shows what a plan costs; FindMyBike routes where evidence goes. The design surface moved from the screen to the consequence.
